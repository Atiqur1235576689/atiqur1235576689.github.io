About

I completed my B.Sc. in Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering at the Bangladesh University of Engineering and Technology, and I currently work as a researcher focused on thermal management. I am applying for PhD study in the United States in this field.

My current research concerns phase change materials (PCM) for thermal management. I am investigating PCM and fin network structures for cooling lithium-ion battery packs under repeated charge and discharge cycles, and I am a co-author on a paper under review at Case Studies in Thermal Engineering on melting enhancement in a PCM-filled enclosure using K-shaped fins, examining how fin angle and arm length ratio affect melting behaviour. I am currently preparing a further paper based on this work.

This research developed from my undergraduate thesis on mooring dynamics for a 15 MW floating offshore wind turbine. During that work, heat transfer and convection became a more significant area of interest than the structural analysis itself, which led to a focus on PCM and thermal systems specifically.

Prior to this, I completed the design of a 175-passenger ferry for the circular waterways around Dhaka, and undertook a three-week internship at the Bangladesh Navy’s Dockyard and Engineering Works in Narayanganj.

In addition to research, I work as a semi-professional photographer and served as Vice President of the BUET Photographic Society. I consider myself people-oriented and value organising initiatives and taking on coordination roles, which I hope to contribute to a research group alongside technical work.

Publication 01 · Thermal energy storage

Melting Enhancement in a
PCM-Filled Enclosure Using K-Shaped Fins

Md Tanvir Anjum Zenith, Adri Das, Shafollo Proshad Gupto, Md. Atiqur Rahaman, Md. Khairul Islam

NAME, BUET, Dhaka · Alan Levin Dept. of Mechanical and Nuclear Engineering, Kansas State University

Case Studies in Thermal EngineeringUnder reviewResults embargoed

A phase change material (PCM) stores a large amount of energy within a narrow temperature range, which makes it suitable for latent-heat thermal storage. However, PCM materials such as lauric acid have low thermal conductivity, comparable to that of a plastic, which limits the rate at which the store can be charged.

Metal fins are a standard method for addressing this limitation, as they conduct heat away from the heated wall and into the material. However, a fin has two simultaneous effects: it conducts heat outward, while also obstructing the buoyancy-driven flow that develops once part of the material has melted. These two effects act in opposition, and the outcome depends on the shape of the fin.

This study examines a K-shaped fin, consisting of two arms meeting at the heated wall, and treats two parameters independently: the angle between the arms, and the distribution of the available fin material between the upper and lower arm. Both parameters are varied while the total fin cross-section is held constant, so that every configuration uses the same amount of aluminium. The study investigates whether fin shape alone, at constant material cost, has a meaningful effect on the charging rate of the store.

My contribution

  • Conceptualization
  • Software · ran the simulations in ANSYS Fluent 2024R1
  • Formal analysis and data curation
  • Visualization · contour plots, efficiency comparisons, and a new melting-uniformity metric introduced in this work
Preliminary indication

Preliminary results indicate that intermediate fin angles combined with a longer lower arm increase melting efficiency. This finding is preliminary; the complete ranking, the metric itself, and all figures remain confidential until the paper is accepted.

Geometry explorer

Select an arm-length ratio and adjust the angle. Every configuration uses the same total fin cross-section, so the variable is the distribution of material rather than its quantity. Angles outside a case’s tested range are flagged, as the arms would extend beyond the enclosure boundary.

Method

Domain and physics

A two-dimensional enclosure represents the latent-heat store: a 50 × 50 mm field of PCM against a 5 mm wall held at a constant 70 °C, with the remaining three boundaries adiabatic. The domain starts uniformly solid at 25 °C and at rest. Melting is captured with the enthalpy–porosity formulation, where the mushy region is treated as a porous zone whose permeability collapses as the liquid fraction falls to zero. Buoyancy enters through the Boussinesq approximation, flow is laminar, and the fins are modelled as conducting solid walls with no slip.

What is varied

  • Angle between the fin arms, swept from 10° to 80°
  • Five splits of fin length between the upper and lower arm
  • A finless enclosure as the reference case
  • Total fin cross-sectional area and thickness held constant throughout

Publication 02 · Offshore structures

Effect of Mooring Spread Angle
on Floating Wind Turbine Motion Response

Dr. Md. Shahidul Islam, Md. Atiqur Rahaman, Md Tanvir Anjum Zenith

Department of Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering, BUET, Dhaka

ManuscriptUnder reviewResults embargoed

A floating wind platform is held in position by catenary chains, which are almost always spread evenly, typically as three lines at 120°, applied uniformly across a wind farm. This is a common default configuration, though it is rarely examined in detail.

Turbines in the interior of an array are sheltered by their neighbours and are exposed to a narrower range of wave directions. Turbines on the outer row do not benefit from this shelter and are exposed to the site’s full directional wave climate, including oblique headings for which a layout designed for head seas has not typically been assessed.

Most published research on mooring geometry considers a fixed wave direction, usually head seas, and reports that tightening or realigning the lines increases platform stiffness. This result is generally assumed to apply across all headings. This study examines whether that assumption holds, and whether the effect of spread angle on platform motion can be separated from wave heading, or whether the two parameters must be considered together. The practical implication is that if this interaction is significant, edge-row and interior turbines within the same farm may not require the same mooring layout.

My contribution

  • Co-developed the methodology, with M.T.A.Z.
  • Built and validated the coupled frequency- and time-domain model in ANSYS AQWA
  • Ran the full 25-case simulation matrix
  • Analysis and visualization; contributed to writing and project administration
Preliminary indication

Preliminary results indicate that tightening the mooring spread consistently reduces surge, but its effect on sway and yaw depends on wave heading: a tighter spread reduces motion in head seas but can increase sway in oblique and beam seas. Heave remains largely insensitive to spread angle. These results suggest that no single spread angle is optimal across all headings. Exact figures remain confidential until the paper is published.

This research developed from my undergraduate thesis, supervised by Dr. Md. Shahidul Islam.

Hydrodynamics

At this hull volume Morison’s equation no longer describes the wave loading well, so the analysis uses three-dimensional diffraction theory. The velocity potential is split into incident, diffracted and radiated components and solved with a sink–source panel method over the mean wetted surface, giving frequency-dependent added mass, radiation damping and wave-exciting forces. For the moored simulation the frequency-domain radiation term is replaced by a convolution over the retardation function, giving the Cummins time-domain equation of motion.

Mooring and validation

Each chain is treated with quasi-static catenary theory including axial stretch. At each step the instantaneous fairlead position dictated by the platform’s six-degree-of-freedom motion updates the catenary parameter and hence the tension in each line; the reaction on the hull is the vector sum of the three, entering the equation of motion as a nonlinear restoring term. The hull was meshed across four panel densities and surge added mass and radiation damping tracked to convergence before fixing element size, then frequency-domain coefficients checked against published values.

Environment held constant across all cases

  • Regular wave, 10 m height and 10 s period
  • Steady current, 0.5 m/s, identical in all 25 runs
  • Steady wind, 10 m/s, applied on the rotor
  • 500 s simulation duration per case

Regular waves, rather than irregular waves, are used deliberately. A single frequency and direction removes spectral content and directional spreading, so that any difference in response can be attributed to the two angles under study. The corresponding limitation is that magnitudes are comparative rather than directly applicable to design; irregular directional seas are identified as the next stage of this research.

The brief

Ikorodu to CMS is a 25 km crossing of the Lagos lagoon. By road, the journey takes 60 to 90 minutes. The water route remains largely unused, not because of distance, but because a ferry serving this route must be safe, affordable and buildable in Nigeria simultaneously in order to operate.

The competition defines this as the design problem. Each constraint reflects an actual condition of the route, and several constraints work against one another: the waterway is 5 m deep, with docking depths of 2.5 m at Ikorodu and 3.0 m at CMS, bridge clearance varies between 6 and 12 m with the tide, and piers are spaced as little as 20 m apart. Draft, air draft and the manoeuvring envelope were therefore fixed before the hull form was developed.

The resulting design is a 28 m aluminium catamaran carrying 200 passengers at 20 knots, powered by battery-electric propulsion, with a door-to-door trip time of 41.5 minutes. This is faster than both the road journey and the existing Ikorodu–Ebute-Ero ferry service, which takes approximately 50 minutes.

Safety case

Demonstrating
safety in the design.

This competition evaluates ferry safety specifically, and ferry accidents are commonly attributed to overloading, marginal stability and inadequate evacuation procedures. The submission was required to demonstrate performance in all three areas rather than assert it.

  • Intact stability passed against IMO A.749(18), HSC multihull and SOLAS II-1/8 criteria, in full load, ballast and lightship conditions

Niger Drift:
a CNG serial-hybrid ferry for the Niger River.

Team Black Pearl · Department of Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering, BUET · Faculty advisor: Professor Dr. Zobair Ibn Awal

International Student Design Competition for a Safe and Affordable Ferry · Entry 2610

Worldwide Ferry Safety AssociationSecond Runner-up, 2026 View certificate →

The brief

Lokoja to Onitsha is a 200 km route along the Niger River. By road, the journey takes 5 to 7 hours; no public transport data exists for this corridor, only records of private car journeys. This year’s competition brief was shaped by a specific development: Nigeria removed its fuel subsidy in 2023, substantially increasing the cost of diesel-powered transport.

This factor significantly influenced the design approach. Battery-electric propulsion was the first option considered, but a preliminary weight assessment ruled it out: covering the route requires approximately 7,700 kWh per trip, and the battery mass required to store this energy, between 66 and 108 tonnes, would be comparable to the vessel’s own displacement of 105.9 tonnes. CNG propulsion was selected instead, consistent with Nigeria’s Presidential Initiative on Compressed Natural Gas, using a serial hybrid system in which onboard gas generators supply the primary power and a smaller battery provides continuity and emergency capacity.

The route presented additional constraints, including a docking depth of 0.89 m at Lokoja compared with 5 m at Onitsha, the absence of an established shipyard at either location, and a stretch of river known for log strikes and occasional piracy. These factors directly informed the hull scantlings and the safety plan.

Propulsion selection

The propulsion decision

Fully electric propulsion was the initial option considered, and it was excluded for a specific technical reason: at the ferry’s required power demand, the battery needed to cover a 200 km trip would weigh approximately as much as the hull itself. CNG generators were selected to supply the primary power instead, with a smaller 280 kWh battery providing continuity and emergency load, following the same serial-hybrid principle used in hybrid vehicles, adapted here for a river rather than a road application.

The safety implications of carrying compressed gas were addressed as design constraints from the outset. The cylinders are positioned more than 2 m from the stern in accordance with the IGF Code, isolated from passenger spaces with natural ventilation, and monitored by five infrared methane detectors connected to automatic solenoid shut-off valves.

Distinctive features

  • LiDAR and forward-looking sonar for real-time obstacle and depth-change detection, addressing the shifting bed and floating logs of the lower Niger
  • A hydrokinetic turbine mounted between the demihulls, using the flow channelled by the hulls as the vessel moves
  • Two non-lethal anti-piracy water cannons and two dedicated crew members, addressing a documented risk on this stretch of river
  • Tiltable, shear-pinned propellers that lift automatically on impact and rotate 90° for cleaning in muddy water
  • A proposed floating CNG pontoon at Lokoja, as no shore-based refuelling platform currently exists there
  • A digital twin monitoring hull stress, CNG generator condition and hydrodynamics in service

Research · Thermal management

Iso-volume fin geometries
for PCM battery cooling.

Ongoing · started August 2026

ANSYS FluentOngoing

The question

A phase change material and fin arrangement may perform well in a single test yet fail in service, since a battery pack is not charged only once but undergoes thousands of charge, discharge and rest cycles over its operating life. This project addresses a question that most single-cycle studies do not answer: whether a fin shape that manages heat effectively in the first cycle continues to perform as well after many repeated cycles.

The full title of the study is Iso-Volume Comparison of Fin Geometries for PCM-Based Battery Thermal Management: Shape Ranking under Repeated Charge and Discharge Cycling. The term “iso-volume” refers to the constraint that ensures a fair comparison: each fin arrangement tested is assigned the same amount of metal. As a result, any difference in performance between shapes can be attributed to geometry rather than to a difference in material allocation.

The comparison is conducted in ANSYS Fluent, tracking the proportion of PCM that remains melted from one cycle to the next. An arrangement may perform well in the first cycle but lose this advantage over time if the material does not fully resolidify during the rest period, a failure mode that a single-cycle study would not detect.

Why it matters

  • Extends the enclosure work behind paper 01 to a cyclic, load-driven setting
  • A battery pack must both absorb heat during discharge and reject it before the next cycle, which presents a more demanding problem than a single melting event
  • Fixing the fin volume isolates shape as the variable under study, rather than allowing it to be confounded with material cost

Undergraduate thesis:
edge-turbine mooring dynamics.

Supervisor: Dr. Md. Shahidul Islam · Department of Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering, BUET

Apr 2025 – Jun 2026Complete

The motivation

The turbines within a floating wind farm do not all experience identical conditions. Turbines toward the interior of the array are sheltered by their neighbours and are exposed to a narrower range of wave directions. Turbines on the outer row do not benefit from this shelter; they are exposed to the site’s full directional wave climate and consequently reach their motion-based shutdown limits earlier.

This thesis develops a coupled frequency- and time-domain model of the IEA 15 MW reference turbine on the UMaine VolturnUS-S semi-submersible platform in ANSYS AQWA, validated against published data, to examine the extent to which this edge-turbine penalty can be addressed through the mooring system, specifically through the angle between the mooring lines.

Subsequent development

The complete method, the 25-case simulation matrix, and the load-case explorer are presented in a manuscript that is currently under review. This entry links directly to that page rather than duplicating its content.

The brief

NAME 338 assigns each pair of students a complete ship design, covering the full design spiral rather than an isolated calculation: hull form, hydrostatics, strength, stability, powering and propulsion, through to an assessment of economic viability. This project addressed that brief for a 175-passenger ferry on the Mirpur–Shyampur route, part of Dhaka’s circular waterways network.

The route is 16 km in each direction (8.64 nautical miles). A basis ship, an existing 200-passenger vessel with published particulars, was used to establish the initial ratio analysis. The L/B, B/T, D/T and B/D ratios from that vessel were adapted for the target capacity and route, then refined through the design spiral until the hull, structure, stability and powering were consistent with one another.

  • Hydrostatics and stability across lightship, lightship-ready and fully loaded departure conditions
  • Scantling calculation to ABS rules, midship section, longitudinal construction and shell expansion

  • Propeller design, power calculation, and rudder sizing to DNV/GL practice
  • Engine and gearbox selection, engine foundation and shaft arrangement

Overview

This was a three-week industrial attachment at Dockyard & Engineering Works Ltd (DEW), a Bangladesh Navy shipyard in Narayanganj, undertaken alongside the design and simulation work carried out at university. While design work describes how a system should behave, an attachment at a working shipyard demonstrates the practical processes involved in constructing and maintaining it.

DEW was established in 1926, making it one of the oldest shipyards in the region, and has been operated by the Bangladesh Navy since 2006. The yard covers approximately 22 acres with a 900 ft riverfront, can dock vessels of up to 1,500 tonnes and 88.4 m in length, and produces approximately 2,000 tonnes of steelwork annually, including patrol craft, landing craft, tugs, tankers, passenger and Ro-Ro ferries, survey vessels and pontoons.

The attachment covered several of the yard’s workshops, with training focused on four areas: marine propulsion systems, shipboard air conditioning, thermal management of engine rooms, and CNC plate cutting, corresponding respectively to the machine shop, the HVAC and engine-room repair bays, and the plater shop.

Organizing and
taking responsibility.

Over four years, several themes have recurred: a term as class representative, a fest that led to friendships which continue to shape my work, a period of disagreement with the administration that developed into a larger movement, and, subsequently, a return to organisational roles on a smaller scale.

First year

I served as Class Representative for my department in my first year, which remains one of the most valuable experiences of my university education, not primarily due to the title, but due to the responsibilities it required. A Class Representative must maintain a working relationship with every classmate and with the representatives of every other department, and must often mediate between positions that neither side fully controls: communicating a professor’s decision to a group of classmates without generating resentment toward the professor, and equally often advocating to a professor on behalf of a position the class supported. This first year of negotiation established a foundation for much of what followed.

Second year

My second year was the busiest of the four and, in my assessment, the most productive. It began with organising the reception for the incoming junior batch largely independently, including sponsorship, budget and logistics. Around the same period, a small group of us organised the second Entrance Day for our own batch, combining a cultural program with a charity visit to a rural school. This reception led to my involvement with a group of 17th-batch seniors organising that year’s NAME Fest, an event that does not occur every year and depends on available budget. They required someone to coordinate between their batch and mine, and, having observed my recent work, invited me to join the organising team, primarily in a logistics capacity.

This fest introduced most of the people who remain significant to my current work. One senior I became close to during this period, Adri Das, continues to be a research collaborator; we are co-authors on the PCM fin paper. Around the same time, I met two 18th-batch seniors, Nazmus Akib and Yasir Arafat, who became close friends during my time at BUET. Akib was particularly influential in introducing me to photography (further detail is provided on the Photography page).

During the same year, I did not continue as Class Representative, as the administration introduced a rule limiting the position to a one-year term, officially intended to broaden participation in the role. Some context is relevant here: in 2019, prior to my enrolment, a BUET student was killed on campus by members of the then-ruling Awami League’s student wing, following a political dispute. This event led to significant protests, a campus-wide ban on student politics, and a widely held view, which I share, that a STEM university should not host party politics; this view has remained influential on campus since. In this context, many students interpreted the new rule differently from how it was presented: the Class Representative network functioned as an informal student leadership structure, and limiting its continuity made this structure more difficult to sustain, which was seen as advantageous to a Students’ Welfare administration widely regarded as politically aligned with the government of the time. In response, a group of us established an informal, batch-based leadership structure outside the official system, without representation on any university committee, but functioning as a genuine channel of communication between students.

Second and third year

This informal structure sustained the response to the administration through my second and third years, continuing through the July 2024 movement at the national level. I was directly involved on several occasions, and photographing the protests was one area in which my interests in organising and photography converged. During this period, I was also significantly involved in NAME Week 2024, organised by the 18th batch under Yasir Arafat, although this was not formally an event for my own batch.

Following the graduation of the 18th batch, shortly before the change of government that August, this phase of organisational work concluded. I subsequently stepped back from the batch-representative role and redirected my attention to coursework, competitions and research.

Subsequent period

The period that followed involved multiple concurrent activities rather than a single focus: two WFSA campaigns with Black Pearl, my undergraduate thesis, the ferry design project, an individual research line, and regular coursework, largely overlapping rather than sequential. During this period, a small group of us also managed the dormitory’s dining committee for one month, responsible for sourcing, budgeting and serving food for approximately 550 people daily, which provided practical experience in negotiation. I also joined the Rag Concert organising committee in my final year, following earlier experience photographing the same concerts as an underclassman; the concert is open to BUET students and alumni but restricted to individuals without a university affiliation, and my responsibility was managing access at the main entrance for the duration of the event.

I subsequently led the cultural program for the 2026 NAME Fest, the same fest I had previously supported in a logistics capacity as an underclassman.

What this experience contributed

  • Negotiating between parties with limited mutual trust, initially between students and faculty, and subsequently between students and administration
  • Securing sponsorship from alumni and local businesses, and managing a fixed budget effectively
  • Maintaining a position under institutional pressure, and recognising when a period of advocacy has concluded
  • Developing working relationships that extended beyond their original context; two current research collaborators were initially connections made through fest organisation
  • Managing events with fixed public schedules and time-sensitive audience expectations
  • Maintaining access control under pressure at an event entrance

Across all of these roles, alongside coursework, competitions and research running in parallel, I have made a consistent effort to ensure that none of them compromised my academic performance. Managing several demanding commitments at the same time, rather than in sequence, is something I consider a core strength.
