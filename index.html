About

I completed my B.Sc. in Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering at the Bangladesh University of Engineering and Technology, and now work as a researcher focused on thermal management. I’m applying for PhD study in the United States in this field.

My current work is on phase change materials (PCM) for thermal management. I’m investigating PCM and fin network structures for cooling lithium-ion battery packs under repeated charge/discharge cycles, and I’m a co-author on a paper under review at Case Studies in Thermal Engineering on melting enhancement in a PCM-filled enclosure using K-shaped fins, looking at how fin angle and arm length ratio change melting behaviour. I’m actively writing up a new paper from this line of work now.

This grew out of my undergraduate thesis on mooring dynamics for a 15 MW floating offshore wind turbine, where heat transfer and convection kept surfacing as the more interesting problem underneath the structural one, which is what pulled me toward PCM and thermal systems specifically.

Before that I designed a 175-passenger ferry for the circular waterways around Dhaka, and interned for three weeks at the Bangladesh Navy’s Dockyard and Engineering Works in Narayanganj.

Outside the research, I’m a semi-professional photographer and served as Vice President of the BUET Photographic Society. I’m deeply people-oriented and enjoy organising initiatives and taking on front-facing coordination, strengths I want to carry into a research group, not just a lab bench.

Publication 01 · Thermal energy storage

A good store of heat
that is bad at moving it.

Md Tanvir Anjum Zenith, Adri Das, Shafollo Proshad Gupto, Md. Atiqur Rahaman, Md. Khairul Islam

NAME, BUET, Dhaka · Alan Levin Dept. of Mechanical and Nuclear Engineering, Kansas State University

Case Studies in Thermal EngineeringUnder reviewResults embargoed

A phase change material holds a large amount of energy in a narrow temperature band, which makes it attractive for latent-heat thermal storage. The problem is getting heat in and out. Lauric acid conducts heat about as well as a plastic, so a store that holds a lot charges slowly.

Metal fins are the standard fix: they carry heat away from the hot wall and deeper into the material. But a fin does two things at once. It conducts heat outward, and it sits in the way of the buoyancy-driven flow that develops once part of the material has melted. Those two effects pull in opposite directions, and which one wins depends on the shape of the fin.

This study takes a K-shaped fin — two arms meeting at the heated wall — and treats it as two independent knobs: the angle between the arms, and how the available fin material is split between the upper and lower arm. Both are varied while the total fin cross-section is held fixed, so every configuration uses the same amount of aluminium. The question is whether shape alone, at constant material cost, meaningfully changes how fast the store charges.

My contribution

  • Conceptualization
  • Software · ran the simulations in ANSYS Fluent 2024R1
  • Formal analysis and data curation
  • Visualization · contour plots, efficiency comparisons, and a new melting-uniformity metric introduced in this work
Preliminary indication

Intermediate fin angles paired with a longer lower arm raised melting efficiency. That is a direction, not a result — the full ranking, the metric itself, and every figure stay embargoed until the paper is accepted.

Geometry explorer

Pick an arm-length ratio and drag the angle. Every configuration uses the same total fin cross-section, so what changes is distribution, not quantity. Angles outside a case’s tested range are flagged — the arms would reach the enclosure boundary.

Method

Domain and physics

A two-dimensional enclosure represents the latent-heat store: a 50 × 50 mm field of PCM against a 5 mm wall held at a constant 70 °C, with the remaining three boundaries adiabatic. The domain starts uniformly solid at 25 °C and at rest. Melting is captured with the enthalpy–porosity formulation, where the mushy region is treated as a porous zone whose permeability collapses as the liquid fraction falls to zero. Buoyancy enters through the Boussinesq approximation, flow is laminar, and the fins are modelled as conducting solid walls with no slip.

What is varied

  • Angle between the fin arms, swept from 10° to 80°
  • Five splits of fin length between the upper and lower arm
  • A finless enclosure as the reference case
  • Total fin cross-sectional area and thickness held constant throughout

Publication 02 · Offshore structures

The turbines on the edge
see a different sea.

Dr. Md. Shahidul Islam, Md. Atiqur Rahaman, Md Tanvir Anjum Zenith

Department of Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering, BUET, Dhaka

ManuscriptUnder reviewResults embargoed

A floating wind platform is held on station by catenary chains, and those chains are almost always spread evenly — three lines at 120°, the same layout across the whole farm. It is a reasonable default. It is also rarely questioned.

Turbines in the interior of an array sit in the wake of their neighbours and meet a narrowed, filtered range of wave directions. Turbines on the outer row do not. They take the site’s full directional wave climate head-on, including the oblique headings that a layout tuned for head seas was never checked against.

Most published work on mooring geometry fixes the wave direction, usually at head seas, and reports that tightening or realigning the lines stiffens the platform. That result is normally treated as if it carries over to every heading. This study asks whether it does — whether the effect of spread angle on platform motion can be separated from wave heading at all, or whether the two have to be chosen together. The design consequence is concrete: if the interaction matters, edge-row and interior turbines in the same farm have no particular reason to share a mooring layout.

My contribution

  • Co-developed the methodology, with M.T.A.Z.
  • Built and validated the coupled frequency- and time-domain model in ANSYS AQWA
  • Ran the full 25-case simulation matrix
  • Analysis and visualization; contributed to writing and project administration
Preliminary indication

Tightening the mooring spread consistently reduces surge, but its effect on sway and yaw depends on wave heading: a tighter spread helps in head seas but can work against sway control in oblique and beam seas. Heave stays largely insensitive throughout. The takeaway is that no single spread angle is best across all headings — the exact numbers and figures stay embargoed until the paper is out.

Hydrodynamics

At this hull volume Morison’s equation no longer describes the wave loading well, so the analysis uses three-dimensional diffraction theory. The velocity potential is split into incident, diffracted and radiated components and solved with a sink–source panel method over the mean wetted surface, giving frequency-dependent added mass, radiation damping and wave-exciting forces. For the moored simulation the frequency-domain radiation term is replaced by a convolution over the retardation function, giving the Cummins time-domain equation of motion.

Mooring and validation

Each chain is treated with quasi-static catenary theory including axial stretch. At each step the instantaneous fairlead position dictated by the platform’s six-degree-of-freedom motion updates the catenary parameter and hence the tension in each line; the reaction on the hull is the vector sum of the three, entering the equation of motion as a nonlinear restoring term. The hull was meshed across four panel densities and surge added mass and radiation damping tracked to convergence before fixing element size, then frequency-domain coefficients checked against published values.

Environment held constant across all cases

  • Regular wave, 10 m height and 10 s period
  • Steady current, 0.5 m/s, identical in all 25 runs
  • Steady wind, 10 m/s, applied on the rotor
  • 500 s simulation duration per case

Regular rather than irregular waves are used deliberately. A single frequency and direction strips out spectral content and directional spreading, so any difference in response can be attributed to the two angles under study. The trade-off is that magnitudes are comparative rather than design-transferable, and irregular directional seas are the stated next step.

Worldwide Ferry Safety Association1st place, 2025

The brief

Ikorodu to CMS is 25 km across the Lagos lagoon. By road it takes 60 to 90 minutes. The water route is right there, largely unused, and the reason is not distance — it is that a ferry has to be safe, affordable and buildable in Nigeria all at once, or it does not run.

The competition sets that as the problem. Every constraint is a real one from the route, and they pull against each other: the waterway is 5 m deep with docking depths of 2.5 m at Ikorodu and 3.0 m at CMS, bridge clearance swings between 6 and 12 m with the tide, and piers are as little as 20 m apart. Draft, air draft and manoeuvring envelope were fixed before the hull form was.

The answer was a 28 m aluminium catamaran carrying 200 passengers at 20 knots, battery-electric, with the trip taking 41.5 minutes door to door. That beats the road, and it beats the existing Ikorodu–Ebute-Ero service at around 50 minutes.

Safety case

The part that
actually wins it.

This is a competition about ferry safety, and ferries are lost to overloading, marginal stability and evacuation that does not work under stress. The submission had to demonstrate all three, not assert them.

Stability and subdivision

  • Intact stability passed against IMO A.749(18), HSC multihull and SOLAS II-1/8 criteria, in full load, ballast and lightship conditions
  • Probabilistic damage stability to SOLAS 2009 and MSC.216(82): attained index 0.962 to 0.970 against a required 0.631
  • 129 damage cases run across six zones, including 65 intermediate-stage cases
  • Two-compartment standard confirmed by floodable length analysis, with bulkheads repositioned to suit
  • Longitudinal strength checked in normal and grounding conditions — grounding governs, at 102.5 t·m

Evacuation and seakeeping

  • Evacuation simulated in Pathfinder with a realistic demographic: 15 elderly passengers, 20 children, 5 crew
  • 6.41 minutes with all exits open; 10.23 minutes with the two rear exits blocked by a machinery-space fire
  • Both well inside the 25.07 minute allowance the HSC Code sets for this vessel
  • Seakeeping assessed Beaufort 1 to 4 in head, beam and following seas against ISO 2631 motion sickness limits
  • 8 liferafts at 25 persons each, 250 lifejackets, sprinklers and detection on both decks

Niger Drift:
a CNG ferry for the Niger River.

Team Black Pearl · Department of Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering, BUET · Faculty advisor: Professor Dr. Zobair Ibn Awal

International Student Design Competition for a Safe and Affordable Ferry · Entry 2610

Worldwide Ferry Safety Association3rd place, 2026

The brief

Lokoja to Onitsha is 200 km along the Niger River. By road it takes 5 to 7 hours; there is no public transport data for the corridor at all, only private car journeys. The competition's brief this year was shaped by a specific shock: Nigeria removed its fuel subsidy in 2023, and diesel-powered transport got a lot more expensive overnight.

That single fact reframes the whole design. Battery-electric propulsion was the first option considered, but a preliminary weight check ruled it out: covering the route needs about 7,700 kWh per trip, and the battery mass to store that — 66 to 108 tonnes — would rival the vessel’s own displacement of 105.9 tonnes. The answer was CNG, in line with Nigeria’s own Presidential Initiative on Compressed Natural Gas: a serial hybrid system where onboard gas generators supply the primary power and a smaller battery covers continuity and emergencies.

The route brought its own constraints too: 0.89 m of docking depth at Lokoja against 5 m at Onitsha, no established shipyard at either end, and a stretch of river known locally for log strikes and occasional piracy — both of which shaped the hull scantlings and the safety plan directly.

Why CNG, and what came with it

A fuel choice that

The propulsion decision

Fully electric propulsion was the first option on the table, and the reason it was dropped is concrete rather than ideological: at the ferry’s power demand, the battery needed to cover a 200 km trip would weigh as much as the hull itself. CNG generators supply the primary power instead, with a much smaller 280 kWh battery handling continuity and emergency load — the same serial-hybrid logic hybrid cars use, sized for a river rather than a road.

The safety consequences of carrying compressed gas were treated as design constraints, not afterthoughts: the cylinders sit more than 2 m from the stern per the IGF Code, isolated from passenger spaces with natural ventilation, monitored by five infrared methane detectors wired to automatic solenoid shut-off valves.

Distinctive features

  • LiDAR and forward-looking sonar for real-time obstacle and depth-change detection — the lower Niger shifts its bed and carries floating logs
  • Hydrokinetic turbine mounted between the demihulls, drawing on the flow the hulls funnel through as the vessel moves
  • Two non-lethal anti-piracy water cannons plus two defensive crew, addressing a documented risk on this stretch of river
  • Tiltable, shear-pinned propellers that lift automatically on impact and rotate 90° for cleaning in muddy water

Research · Thermal management

Iso-volume fin geometries
for PCM battery cooling.

Ongoing · started August 2026

ANSYS FluentOngoing

The question

A phase change material and fin arrangement can look good in a single test and still fail in service, because a battery pack is never charged once. It cycles — charge, discharge, rest, repeat — for thousands of cycles over its life. This project asks a simple question that most single-cycle studies can’t answer: does a fin shape that manages heat well on cycle one still manage it on cycle fifty?

The full title is Iso-Volume Comparison of Fin Geometries for PCM-Based Battery Thermal Management: Shape Ranking under Repeated Charge and Discharge Cycling. “Iso-volume” is the constraint that makes the comparison fair: every fin arrangement tested is given the same amount of metal. So when one shape outperforms another, the difference is the geometry itself, not one design getting a bigger material budget than its rivals.

The comparison runs in ANSYS Fluent, tracking how much of the PCM stays melted from one cycle to the next. An arrangement can look excellent in the first cycle and still lose that edge later, if the material never fully re-solidifies during the rest period — a failure mode a single-cycle study would never catch.

Why it matters

  • Extends the enclosure work behind paper 01 into a cyclic, load-driven setting
  • A battery pack has to both absorb heat during discharge and reject it before the next cycle — a harder problem than melting once
  • Fixing the fin volume isolates shape as the variable, rather than confounding it with material cost

Undergraduate thesis:
the turbines on the edge.

Supervisor: Dr. Md. Shahidul Islam · Department of Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering, BUET

Apr 2025 – Jun 2026Complete

The motivation

A floating wind farm is not one turbine's problem repeated many times over. Turbines toward the interior of the array sit in each other's wake and see a narrowed, filtered range of wave directions. Turbines on the outer row don't get that shelter — they take the site's full directional wave climate head-on, and reach their motion-based shutdown limits earlier as a result.

The thesis builds a coupled frequency- and time-domain model of the IEA 15 MW reference turbine on the UMaine VolturnUS-S semi-submersible platform in ANSYS AQWA, validated against published data, to ask how much of that edge-turbine penalty can be designed around through the mooring system alone — specifically, the angle between the mooring lines.

What this thesis became

The full method, the 25-case simulation matrix, and the load-case explorer are written up as a manuscript, currently under review. Rather than duplicate that page, this entry points to it directly.

Project · Ship design

Design of a 175-passenger
ferry for Dhaka's waterways.

With Md. Kawsar Mhamud Zidan · Supervisor: Dr. Mir Tareque Ali · Department of Naval Architecture and Marine Engineering, BUET

NAME 338, Ship Design Project and PresentationComplete · Oct 2024

The brief

NAME 338 hands each pair of students a full ship to design end to end — not one calculation in isolation, but the whole spiral: hull form, hydrostatics, strength, stability, powering, propulsion, right through to whether the numbers make economic sense. This project took that brief for a 175-passenger ferry on the Mirpur–Shyampur route, part of Dhaka's circular waterways network.

The route is 16 km each way (8.64 nautical miles). A basis ship — an existing 200-passenger vessel with published particulars — anchored the early ratio analysis: L/B, B/T, D/T and B/D ratios from that vessel were carried across and adjusted for the target capacity and route, then refined through the design spiral until the hull, structure, stability and powering all closed together.

What the project covered

  • Basis-ship ratio analysis, principal dimensions, lines plan and table of offsets
  • Hydrostatics and stability across lightship, lightship-ready and fully loaded departure conditions
  • Scantling calculation to ABS rules, midship section, longitudinal construction and shell expansion
  • Resistance by the Holtrop & Mennen and Van Oortmerssen methods
  • Propeller design, power calculation, and rudder sizing to DNV/GL practice
  • Engine and gearbox selection, engine foundation and shaft arrangement
  • HSE analysis (ventilation, smoke extraction) and an economic feasibility study

Route and operations

Sized for the run,
not just the passengers.

Duty cycle

Mirpur to Shyampur is 16 km each way. At the 12-knot service speed, a round trip runs about 2 hours, and the vessel was sized around seven round trips across a 14-hour operating day — the schedule that set the fuel, fresh water and provisioning loads carried into the deadweight estimate.

Economics

An NPV analysis over a 25-year service life, at a 12% discount rate, returned a positive net present value against an estimated initial cost of roughly BDT 14.4 crore (~USD 1.3 million at the time). The project treated economic viability as a real design constraint, not an afterthought bolted on at the end.

Work experience

working shipyard.

Dockyard & Engineering Works Ltd · Bangladesh Navy · Narayanganj, Bangladesh

Industrial attachment3 weeks

What this was

A three-week industrial attachment at Dockyard & Engineering Works Ltd (DEW), a Bangladesh Navy shipyard in Narayanganj, alongside the design and simulation work at university. Design work explains how something should behave; a shipyard floor shows what actually goes into building and maintaining it.

DEW traces back to 1926 — among the oldest shipyards in the region — and has been run by the Bangladesh Navy since 2006. The yard covers close to 22 acres with a 900 ft riverfront, can dock vessels up to 1,500 tonnes and 88.4 m in length, and turns out roughly 2,000 tonnes of steelwork a year across patrol craft, landing craft, tugs, tankers, passenger and Ro-Ro ferries, survey vessels and pontoons.

The attachment moved across several of the yard's workshops, with training focused on four areas: marine propulsion systems, shipboard air conditioning, thermal management of engine rooms, and CNC plate cutting — work that runs through the machine shop, the HVAC and engine-room repair bays, and the plater shop respectively.
